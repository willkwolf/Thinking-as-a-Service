# A framework for using Theory of Constraints thinking processes and tools to complement qualitative system dynamics modelling

MAIN ARTICLE

A framework for using Theory of Constraints thinking processes and tools to complement qualitative system dynamics modelling

Victoria J. Mabin<sup>*</sup> and Robert Y. Cavana

Wellington School of Business and Government, Victoria University of Wellington, Wellington, New Zealand

## Abstract

While the tools currently used for qualitative system dynamics (Qual SD) modelling are very powerful in providing a holistic perspective and a framework for understanding complexity and change, they are often not explicitly designed to build and implement long-term solutions based on that understanding. The Theory of Constraints (TOC) thinking processes and tools focus on these important aspects of the decision-making process. We provide a six-stage framework combining selected tools from TOC’s thinking process and Qual SD’s modelling process to provide a more rigorous systems thinking change process. We illustrate the methods and component tools via a case study on a complex societal issue (sale of alcohol in New Zealand supermarkets) developed for teaching purposes. We demonstrate the value of the framework, highlighting complementarities between tools, and show how specific insights emerged using each of the tools, with more insights resulting from using Qual SD and TOC collectively than separately.

## INTRODUCTION

Societal problems pose challenges for policy makers due in part to the multiplicity of stakeholders and uncertainty surrounding the likely effects of any proposed changes. Systems thinking tools can help by representing multiple viewpoints, and mapping cause and effect relationships in such situations. System dynamics (SD) is a methodological approach to managerial decision making suitable for complex problem solving, developed as a computer-aided simulation approach by Jay Forrester (1961). It has been further developed by a broad range of international scholars including Randers (1980), Richardson and Pugh (1981), Roberts et al. (1983), Wolstenholme (1990), Senge (1990), Coyle (1996), Sterman (2000), Ford (2010) and many others. SD has two main approaches: qualitative SD and quantitative SD (see, e.g., Coyle, 2000; Maani & Cavana, 2007; Wolstenholme & Coyle, 1983).

In this paper we focus on the qualitative SD (Qual SD) modelling process, sometimes referred to as ‘systems thinking’ (e.g., Senge, 1990). Systems thinking provides a framework for understanding complexity and change. It is the ability to see things as a whole. The learning outcome from combining systems thinking tools is ‘to provide a better understanding of the dynamic issues, short- and long-term consequences of actions and policies, a better understanding of system behaviour, and possible intervention points in the system to improve behaviour’ (Cavana & Mares, 2004, p. 228).

Qual SD focuses on understanding the connectiveness in systems (i.e., their structure), providing hypotheses about the dynamic consequences of actions and policies (i.e., their behaviour), and multi-loop systems feedback effects on both structure and behaviour, often through recognising systems archetypes and identifying leverage points for further action and change. While SD is generally regarded as a comprehensive approach to complex decision making, it is not explicitly designed for focusing on goal-oriented thinking, managing constraints, dealing with conflicts and dilemmas, avoiding undesirable consequences and achieving ambitious targets.

On the other hand, the Theory of Constraints (TOC) thinking processes and constituent broad toolkit (TP) have been purposefully designed to focus on these important aspects of the decision-making process (see, e.g., Cox & Schleier, 2010; Dettmer, 2007). TOC originated as a method to dramatically improve production operations and was popularised in the novel The Goal (Goldratt & Cox, 1986), but quickly developed into a systems thinking, management philosophy and methodology with applications in virtually every domain, including public and private sector services, retail, technology, project management, health, education and personal development (Kim et al., 2008). Examples of TOC applications in these many areas are available through the Theory of Constraints International Certification Organization (TOCICO; https://www.tocico.org/page/toc_basics_portal and individual topic portals). The TOC approach is designed to promote goal-oriented thinking, managing constraints, dealing with conflicts and dilemmas, avoiding undesirable consequences and achieving ambitious targets, through its suite of tools that address each stage of the decision-making and implementation process.

An analysis of TOC tools alongside SD and other systems methods using the Mingers and Brocklesby (1997) multimethodology framework has been provided by Davies et al. (2005). This demonstrated the complementarity of TOC tools in terms of the individual TOC tools complementing each other. Complementarities between TOC and other methods have also been explored (see, e.g., Mabin & Davies, 2004; Mabin et al., 2008). Synergies between TOC and Qual SD have been highlighted in several case studies and articles using one or two TOC tools along with Qual SD (e.g., Davies et al., 2004, 2013; Mabin, 1999; Mabin & Davies, 2004; Mabin et al., 2006; Moore, 2009) while, more recently, Johnson (2021) used stakeholder mapping, causal loop diagrams (CLD) and several TOC tools in a multi-framing research study. Koljonen and Reid (1999) investigated using a quantitative SD model to validate TOC TP diagrams, highlighting that with SD only one model is built, whereas TOC uses separate diagrams for the current and future situations. This is partly because of the directional language used in TP diagrams, but also because the TP diagrams incorporate the extra required conditions/changes needed to turn a situation around.

We maintain that combining aspects of Qual SD and TOC in a complementary fashion will result in a more thorough and rigorous decision-making process and more robust fundamental long-term solutions for managing complex problems and issues. The advantage of using multiple tools from both Qual SD and TOC approaches together is that they contribute different perspectives and offset individual methods’ weaknesses for thinking through complex problems, as will be demonstrated in this paper. They foster collaboration and creativity when tackling ill-structured problems by utilising different expertise, personal preferences and experiences. Using multiple frames thus takes advantage of the complementary strengths of different modelling approaches.

Informed by, and informing, these developments, our undergraduate course in Systems Thinking and Decision Making has, over 20 years, combined parts of TOC with Qual SD. We, along with our colleagues, have presented the course in various formats over this time and developed numerous case studies applying both sets of tools. The last piece of assessment typically includes a case study requiring the application of a selection of these methods. The synergy between the methods has always been a notable feature that students remark on.

In this paper, we illustrate how we used a selected range of TOC’s thinking processes and tools to complement the qualitative SD modelling process, to explore a typical complex case developed for educational purposes: alcohol sales in supermarkets and New Zealand’s drinking problem<sup>i</sup> (see Appendix A). The paper will describe the thinking processes and tools covered, propose a systems thinking change process framework combining the two sets of processes, demonstrate their application to this illustrative case, and conclude with insights and recommendations concerning the proposed combined framework.

We briefly outline the Qual SD modelling process and TOC thinking tools and processes used in this paper.

### TOC thinking process tools and protocols

Goldratt (1990, pp. ix–x) explained that the runaway success of The Goal (1986) led many companies to replicate the results, and also exposed two major obstacles, namely that any improvement, no matter how big, is not sufficient in the long term; and secondly, that while The Goal provides brilliant solutions, what is needed is a process that will enable management to generate their own solutions.

He lists three basic questions that any manager should answer when striving for ongoing improvement (Goldratt, 1990, pp. 7–8): What to change? To what to change to [sic]? and How to cause the change? He uses two logic mechanisms— effect–cause–effect reasoning and necessary condition thinking—to explore these. Goldratt and colleagues at the Avraham Y. Goldratt Institute developed these into a codified set of tools showcased in his novel It’s Not Luck (Goldratt, 1994), while others have further developed, refined and explained them—notably Dettmer (1997, 2007).

The first basic question—What to change?—seeks to identify the system’s core problem as the most important place to focus on to achieve improvement. We all tend to concentrate on taking corrective actions that we know how to take, not necessarily concentrating on the problems we should correct and actions needed to correct those problems. Rather than treating symptoms (called undesirable effects (UDEs) in TOC terminology)<sup>ii</sup> of these problems, we need to find the root causes that if addressed would lead to the reduction or elimination of the problems. The ‘core problem’ is often an unresolved dilemma or conflict. What to change to generates solution ideas to this conflict and then tests the best solution—ideally a breakthrough solution—adding complementary actions as needed to ensure the solution achieves the desired results and any undesired side effects are nipped in the bud. How to cause the change identifies and overcomes obstacles that would otherwise block the change.

The original set of TP tools used to address each question consisted of a set of five tools (four trees and an ‘Evaporating Cloud’), shown in Table 1. Note that the Evaporating Cloud straddles two questions: it helps establish the core problem answering the question What to Change?, while its solution starts answering What to Change to?

Goldratt’s original set of three change questions have been augmented by two further questions (Barnard 2010; Cox et al., 2012, p. 34; Mabin & Davies, 2010). At the start of the process has been added—Why change?—to identify the goal and confirm that it is not being met currently, providing a motivation for change. Goldratt’s view was that improvement is always possible, suggesting that this question is unnecessary. However, Dettmer has long been an advocate of starting at this point and has developed the Goal Tree for this stage (Dettmer, 2007, 2011, 2022). He argues that UDEs should be limited to deviations from the conditions needed to achieve the goal, and that the Current Reality Tree should dig down to a few critical root causes (policies, practices or behaviours that lie within the decision maker’s sphere of influence)—rather than to one core problem (Dettmer, 2007, p. 107). A fifth question—How to measure and sustain the change?—addresses rules and measures to lock in new behaviours and sustain the change. The Strategy and Tactics Tree (STT) is now also included in the TP (Scheinkopf, 2010) and other tools are being added over time to the Body of Knowledge (https://www.tocico.org/page/TOCBodyofKnowledge).

The expanded set including the Goal Tree and STT is shown in Figure 2.

These TOC tools are designed to identify root causes and surface assumptions, and develop, test, hone and implement solutions (Cox et al., 2012; Dettmer, 2007; Scheinkopf, 2010). They use two different types of logic: Necessary Condition (NC) and Sufficient Cause (SC), with various logic protocols and checks. NC logic (in order to … we must …) is used in the Goal Tree, Evaporating Cloud and Prerequisite Tree, while SC logic (cause and effect: if … and …, then …) is used in Current and Future Reality Trees and the Negative Branch Reservation process, and Transition Tree. The STT uses both types of logic to provide a full plan from high-level strategy down to tactics to achieve it, stating the conditions required and steps to be taken.

The main TOC thinking process tools, their purpose and logic used (SC, NC) are summarised below:

- Goal Tree (GT)—strategic map of destination (NC)

- Current Reality Tree (CRT)—to identify critical root causes of current issues (SC)

- Evaporating Cloud (EC)—to depict dilemmas and generate potential win–win solutions (NC)

- Future Reality Tree (FRT)—to test whether the solution will eliminate or significantly reduce the UDEs (SC)

- Negative Branch Reservation (NBR) process—used when building the FRT to identify potential negative side effects that may arise from the new solution, and mitigate their effects (SC)

- Prerequisite Tree (PRT)—implementation plan for ambitious targets/ interventions (NC)

- Transition Tree (TrT)—a step-by-step plan for ensuring actions are done correctly to achieve desired results (SC)

- Strategy and Tactics Tree (STT)—lays out a full plan for the organisation/ industry (Both SC and NC).

A set of logic checks called the Categories of Legitimate Reservation (CLR) is used to scrutinise trees to enhance their validity. These prompt the modeller to check that the entities (shown in boxes) in a tree are clearly stated and exist, and that the cause–effect links are valid and fully explained. Texts such as Dettmer (2007, ch 2: pp. 31–65) and Scheinkopf (2010) explain TOC logic clearly, and provide examples, further explanation and advice. Dettmer provides a self-scrutiny checklist for the CLR (Dettmer, 2007, p. 65; see Appendix C) and (Dettmer, 1997, p. 339) provides a version in ‘conversational English’ showing how the CLRs can be used in a constructive and non-threatening way to foster productive discussions.

Balderstone (1999) recommended SD modellers use the CLRs to assist in the SD validation process. Cavana and Mares (2004) and Rees et al. (2018) offer somewhat similar logic constructs but they are different in important regards.

## TOWARDS A COMBINED FRAMEWORK

When using methods from different frameworks, one can either merge them into a multi-methodology or apply them separately—usually sequentially—in a complementary multi-framing approach. The main advantage of the latter approach is that this does not invoke paradigm incommensurability (Mingers & Brocklesby, 1997) while allowing insights from one frame to inform the following frame(s). Frames can be deliberately chosen such that strengths of one or more frames compensate for weaknesses or shadows in another frame. This type of multi-framing uses complementarity to advantage (Mabin & Davies, 2004).

The TOC tools naturally complement each other by working through problem identification to solution implementation (Davies et al., 2005). Overall, Qual SD frames have a similar purpose to TOC frames, but focus on the feedback mechanisms inherent in decision making and the longer-term dynamic consequences and behaviour of different structures and actions by stakeholder groups. In this paper we demonstrate how the Qual SD and TOC frames can complement each other by illustrating the strengths and insights each tool can provide in working towards an overall goal.

For undergraduate teaching purposes, we apply a selection of the above tools from Qual SD and TOC, to analyse a case, note insights gained and provide recommendations. We generally guide students to follow a sequence of Qual SD tools first and then TOC tools, though iteration back and forth is possible and often helpful.

This combined process can be illustrated as in Figure 3, which shows the various stages and their suggested sequence (in solid arrows), with a possible sequence indicated by a dashed arrow. While we indicate (dashed arrow) that the initial Qual SD analysis (Stage 2) could be undertaken before the Change Questions 1 and 2 of the TOC analysis (Stage 3), this is not necessary and they can be undertaken at the same time, revealing different insights and understandings based on the information collected in the preliminary stage of the process. Insights into both these stages can be used to revise the CLDs and BOT charts in Stage 4, before the TOC analysis of Change Question 3 and implementation stages (Stages 5 and 6).

In deciding which TOC TP tools to use in the combined method, we have started with the original three ‘change questions’, but added in the GT mid-way through the process rather than at the start. This reflects both our recognition of the great value of the GT in adding clarity but also our experience in situations when the researchers’ knowledge of the broader context is insufficient at the outset to construct a meaningful GT. A preliminary GT may be constructed at the outset as Dettmer (2022) suggests, and then revisited once the understanding of the situation is sufficiently developed through the modelling process as shown here. Moore (2009) demonstrates the effectiveness of the GT to guide her research, constructing a skeleton at the start and revisiting it several times during the project. Kimani (2015, p. 279) experienced some difficulties with the GT, observing that it requires participants to put aside normal activities, verbalise overall goals that we do not normally stop and think about, and display an ability to see the wood for the trees; different participants will raise different critical success factors (CSFs) and NCs that the tree builder must reconcile using their knowledge of the system. Constructing the GT in the middle helps to navigate these issues.

In this paper we demonstrate the Qual SD and TOC systems thinking tools using an illustrative case study approach to ‘systematically gather in-depth information on a single entity’ (Cavana et al., 2001, p. 112). In our case study, the ‘single entity’ is the duopoly of supermarket chains in New Zealand (NZ) selling alcohol to the general public on full display, and the subject of repeated calls by medical bodies to ban sales of alcohol in supermarkets (e.g. Stewart 2017). Case study research usually provides rich deep analysis, although the findings are not necessarily generalisable but useful to encourage deeper learning (Yin, 2009). Nevertheless, case studies (both qualitative and quantitative) are widely regarded as useful for teaching managerial decision making.

While all the tools listed in the combined systems thinking framework could be used in a case study, the illustration here focuses on the range of tools taught on this undergraduate course and shows how these may be used together in an illustrative case analysis. A summary of the analysis and examples of some of the diagrams developed are presented below.

### Preliminary analysis

#### Situation summary

The NZ drinking problem has featured prominently in the news in recent times, with a particular focus on sales of alcohol in supermarkets (e.g. Stewart, 2017). The NZ Medical Association argued that sales in the supermarket, alongside bread and milk, send the wrong signals that alcohol is an everyday consumable product. Such implicit normalisation of alcohol contrasts with their view that alcohol is a psychoactive drug that is toxic to human tissue, being a carcinogenic and aggressogenic drug. Supermarkets use heavy discounting of alcoholic products. Easy access and cheaper pricing ‘encourage’ more young people in NZ to get drunk and engage in ‘binge drinking’, with consequential negative medical and social outcomes. This suggests that substantial conflicts exist regarding the commercial and human wellbeing effects of alcohol sales in NZ society. See Appendix A for the teaching case incorporating a summary of alcohol statistics.

In addition, McBride-Henry et al. (2020, pp. 21–22) have summarised the consumer alcohol exposure problems in NZ supermarkets (see that article for their references relating to these extracts) as follows:

### Initial Qual SD analysis

#### Organising question

Following on from a review of the case materials (shown in Appendix A) and some initial desk research, we initially decided on the following organising question for this case. This helps set the focus, domain and boundaries for the investigation:

How can the harm caused by excessive alcohol consumption in New Zealand be reduced?

However, following a shift in purpose of this teaching case to better illustrate how the TOC thinking process tools can be used to complement the Qual SD modelling process, we narrowed the scope of the organising question to:

How can harm caused by the sale of alcohol in New Zealand supermarkets be reduced?

The Current Reality Tree (CRT) (discussed later) helped to clarify the nature and purpose of the study. It showed that the supply of alcohol leads to ready access for consumption … which leads to the adverse health and social impacts, resulting in calls for change from medical and health professionals. Hence the organising question is better to focus on reducing the availability/ease of access to alcohol through supermarkets rather than alcohol consumption. In this way, the CRT helped clarify what we are aiming at (the better outcome) and why, reinforcing the selection of our final organising question for the illustrative case in the paper.

#### Stakeholder analysis

An analysis of the main stakeholder groups in the case (following, e.g., Elias & Cavana, 2000; Elias et al., 2002; Freeman, 1984) are summarised in the stakeholder map shown in Figure 4. Examples of the stakes and interests of the main stakeholders are provided in Table 2.

Figure 4 and Table 2 display the wide variety of stakeholder groups who either influence or are influenced by alcohol sales in NZ. Of particular concern are the social effects on consumers and the general public, and the health-related concerns expressed by health sector organisations and medical agencies.

Meanwhile, the liquor industry and supermarkets are focusing on sales growth and profit maximisation from increasing alcohol sales. Related legislative changes have been dormant in recent years in NZ, although more recently there was an important Sale and Supply of Alcohol (Harm Minimisation) Amendment Bill discussed in the NZ Parliament, which would have resulted in significant changes related to the sale and consumption of alcohol if introduced. Unfortunately, the Bill was defeated in April 2023, although a related Community Participation Bill was passed later in the year, which dealt with some of the issues raised.

Most of the major stakeholders concerned with the sale and consumption of alcohol in NZ are identified in Table 2, sufficient for the present illustrative case. For a formal SD study, the stakeholder analysis could involve a more thorough analysis of the stakes, interests and concerns of the major stakeholders within each stakeholder group. In other Qual SD studies in practice (e.g., Cavana et al., 2007), a stakeholder map with some brief discussion of the stakes or interests of the main stakeholders has been found to be sufficient for the analysis to proceed.

#### Initial causal loop diagram

The CLD was initially constructed from student assignment contributions and class group model building activities (e.g., Andersen et al., 1997, 2007; Cavana et al., 1999, 2007; Maani & Cavana, 2007; Scott et al., 2016; Vennix, 1996).

However, for the purposes of this paper, which is to demonstrate the benefits of combining TOC with Qual SD methods, we have reconstructed the ‘initial’ CLD derived from the causal logic contained in identifying the UDEs and situation summary outlined above. In addition, the CLD has been constructed to explain the dynamic behaviour of the main variables identified in the BOT chart (Figure 5). This illustrative CLD is presented in Figure 6, which also shows the main feedback loops present in the current system involving the sale of alcohol in supermarkets in NZ. The initial CLD was constructed by first identifying the causal logic underpinning the first reinforcing loop (R1).

Briefly, the reinforcing loop R1 shows the compounding effects of the current normalisation of alcohol sales in supermarkets (i.e., displaying alcohol for sale alongside grocery and other items in the store) on alcohol demand and supermarket sales; loop R2 shows the growth loop of more alcohol sales in supermarkets leading to greater alcohol consumption in NZ then to higher future demand for alcohol; loop R3 illustrates the reinforcing effects of higher alcohol sales volume leading to higher revenue and profits in supermarkets and more marketing of alcohol products (i.e., wine and beer), thus further stimulating the demand for alcohol; loop R4 illustrates the effects of higher supermarket alcohol sales leading to bulk purchasing discounts by the duopoly supermarket chain in NZ, thus leading to lower alcohol purchase prices, compared with their competitors in NZ, and then onto lower supermarket alcohol prices which further stimulate the demand for alcohol from supermarkets. Finally, loop R5 indicates the higher supermarket alcohol sales volume and revenue lead onto higher profits, which enables supermarkets to cut their prices even lower, thus further stimulating demand and subsequent alcohol sales!

Against these economic benefits for supermarkets in NZ, Figure 6 also summarises Government responses to the harmful health and social effects of normal and excessive alcohol consumption on people and society in NZ. These include the balancing loop B1 operating on the health impacts of hazardous (binge drinking) consumption of alcohol adding to the health costs in society, leading to additional pressure on Government by the public to further regulate alcohol sales (including the sale of alcohol in supermarkets), and leading after a period of time to changes in Government regulations controlling alcohol sales. An example of this is the excise tax on alcohol, which adds to the alcohol purchase prices by supermarkets and their alcohol sale prices to their customers, which then dampens alcohol demand, supermarket sales volumes and final alcohol consumption by their customers. This balancing loop is repeated to show the Government responses to the adverse hazardous social impacts (loop B2) on people in NZ, including domestic violence, crime, traffic accidents, work disruptions, etc.

### TOC analysis (Change Questions 1 and 2)

#### CQ1: What to change?

##### TOC Current Reality Tree

The CRT is used to explore and explain why gaps in performance exist. In particular, it identifies the root causes that lead to the undesired effects/symptoms/gaps which indicate that the system is not performing as well as desired. While the CLD above provides many of these cause–effect relationships, the CRT adds the reasons (assumptions or conditions) that are necessary and sufficient to explain why a cause leads to an effect. The identification of root causes and the use of sufficiency logic provides focus for ‘solving’ the problem. The CRT is designed to answer the question What to change? There are two main ways of constructing a CRT—the traditional approach is by directly connecting causes and effects working from the UDEs and seeking to identify the likely core problem or a small number of root causes leading to the UDEs. During the process, the cause–effect relationships are checked for sufficiency, clarity, existence, etc. using the CLR (Appendix C). The second approach uses three Evaporating Clouds (EC) to identify the root cause, after which the causal map is constructed (Scheinkopf, 2010).

The links in the CRT come from a combination of the modeller’s mental models, literature, stakeholders and other sources where available. To check if they are reasonable, we use CLRs, reading out aloud, checking with those who know. Where the leap between cause and effect is not obvious to a listener, it is sometimes because the reason isn’t obvious—then we surface and add the assumption connected with a ‘banana’ to explain the link. Other times it is because the leap between cause and effect is too big (referred to as a ‘long arrow’). In these cases, we add intermediate effects (and assumptions as needed). When checking whether the resulting tree is reasonable, we aim for a requisite model (Ashby, 1958) that makes sense and allows the decision maker to move to the next step in the process. We aim to build down far enough to identify the few root causes (or cause) leading to the UDEs, within the span of control or sphere of influence of the decision maker, to provide a focus for action.

We used the traditional approach working with the evidence from the case description to piece together the cause–effect relationships that led to the current reality. The result is shown in Figure 7 and can be read, bottom up, using ‘If … (and …) then …’. The ellipses (nicknamed bananas) indicate that two or more causes are needed for the effect to occur—so are read ‘and’.

[Figure 7](../assets/figure/figure-7.jpg)

FIGURE 7. Current Reality Tree (CRT) for sale of alcohol in NZ supermarkets case.

For example, IF law changes in the 1990s and later have loosened restraints on alcohol sales allowing sales of beer and wine in supermarkets, AND alcoholic beverages are very profitable and work well as a loss leader, THEN supermarkets actively encourage alcohol sales through marketing, placement and heavy discounting.

IF that, THEN alcohol is placed alongside bread and milk in supermarkets.

IF that, AND alcohol is addictive, THEN many adults have become regular, heavy and/or binge drinkers of alcohol.

If that, AND a proportion of heavy drinkers are susceptible to becoming hazardous drinkers, AND messages relating to alcohol risks are small and easily disregarded, THEN 20% adults (≈800k) are hazardous drinkers.

If that, AND alcohol is aggressogenic, THEN NZ has high levels of alcohol-related harm necessitating ED visits.

If that, AND alcohol-related ED admissions cost more than ordinary ED admissions, THEN the NZ health system has elevated costs for ED visits from alcohol-related harm.

We read up the right-hand side as well, until we reach: IF alcohol-related health costs (and use of scarce resources) are high, AND medical professionals view treating avoidable harm as a waste of resources, AND supermarkets are a significant supplier of alcohol, THEN medical and health professionals are extremely concerned about alcohol sales in supermarkets. and IF this AND alcohol-related problems are unlikely to reduce without intervention, THEN medical and health professionals feel compelled to repeatedly and urgently call for a ban on alcohol sales in supermarkets.

In this way, the CRT aims to capture the chain of logic between the current calls from medical professionals to ban supermarket sales of alcohol, and the supposed root causes: the law changes, alcohol as a uniquely profitable product good as a loss leader, NZ’s historical attitude to drinking and the fact that alcohol is addictive.

There are other root causes (entities without an arrow entering them), such as children learning from adults and warning messages being small and easily disregarded.

Some of these root causes suggest avenues for easy change, like making messages larger, while others are often accepted as ‘facts of life’—like alcohol being addictive, but these could also be challenged in the search for a solution.

The UDEs in the original list were used as a starting point but most needed rewording following scrutiny using the CLRs, while others were added, and some were not needed in this focused tree.

Note that each entity is placed in a box and is a complete sentence. This is a point of difference from the CLD with significant implications, as discussed later. There often is a feedback arrow leading from the top back down to intermediate or root cause(s), but there should never be an arrow going both ways between two entities.

Usually when the CRT is read aloud, there are links or entities that are not clear or not valid. These require adjusting. For example, the relationship leading to ‘20% adults …’ begs the question, why this %? That entity could be changed to ‘Too many adults …’ even though we have evidence that 20% of adults in NZ are hazardous drinkers. Some links may require an additional entity, a reason or assumption, added alongside and linked with a banana, until the entities at the tail of the arrows are sufficient together to cause the effect.

In this way the CRT can be checked and adjusted to reflect the current situation and provide a strong base for the next process in TOC, the Evaporating Cloud.

##### TOC Evaporating Cloud—construction

The purpose of an evaporating cloud (EC) is to establish why a conflict (or impasse) exists (helping answer the question of What to change?), and also to identify ways of resolving it with a win–win breakthrough solution (answering the question What to change to?). The EC consists of five boxes, with the conflicting wants in boxes D and D′,<sup>iii</sup> the needs that they are satisfying in boxes B and C, and a common objective in box A. An EC for the case is presented in Figure 8, depicting the national-level conflict between the views expressed by medical experts and the supermarkets.

FIGURE 8. Evaporating Cloud—at a national level.

[Figure 8](../assets/figure/figure-8.jpg)

The EC was constructed from right to left, starting with the dilemma of whether to ban supermarket sales of alcohol (D) or continue to allow sales (D′). Once completed, the EC is read from left to right, using necessary condition logic, as follows:

NZ’s goal is to (A) Improve NZ’s overall wellbeing now and in the future. According to the medical experts, in order to (A) Improve NZ’s overall wellbeing now and in the future, NZ society must (B) Reduce alcohol-related harm in NZ, and in order to do that the NZ Government must (D) Ban alcohol sales in supermarkets.

On the other hand, according to the supermarkets, in order to achieve this overall objective, the supermarkets must (C) Maintain their profitability, and to do this the NZ Government must (D′) Continue to allow alcohol sales in supermarkets. Hence the conflict!

This diagram neatly portrays the conflict as the result of two sides which are both needed to achieve the common objective, but are driven to opposing actions. This particular EC clearly focuses on the main issue evident in the case—namely the opposing views expressed by the supermarkets and the medical experts.

Many other valid ECs were produced by the researchers and by the students depending on the perspective taken, ranging from the wider societal perspective above to a personal perspective (wanting to drink regularly in order to feel relaxed, versus not drinking regularly in order to satisfy the need to avoid health and social issues in the long term). Other ECs captured supermarkets’ dilemmas—for example, over continuing alcohol sales in order to maintain their revenue stream (Financial) versus stopping the sale of alcohol in order to maintain their reputation in the community (community social responsibility, CSR).

Taking these various ECs together led to an understanding of the fuller picture, which informed the construction of a Goal Tree, stating what the ideal would be at a broad societal—national—level.

> <sup>iii</sup>D′ is read ‘Not D’ and is either the opposite of D or mutually exclusive.

##### TOC Goal Tree

The purpose of a Goal Tree (GT) is to clearly define a system’s overall goal, and the factors necessary to achieve the goal. It is a very useful tool for gaining agreement on a shared goal and visualising this desired ideal future.

The process starts with a statement of the goal, along with critical success factors or criteria (CSF) and NC for the CSF. The GT uses necessity logic (‘In order to attain the goal, we must have CSF …’), and the CSF should be limited to two to three ‘showstoppers’, rather than a shopping list of nice-to-haves. Below each CSF we show the NC that must be met if the CSF is to be achieved. Note that even though these NCs are at the bottom of the GT they are not less important—indeed, they are arguably more important. CSFs or NCs that are not currently met indicate areas that need attention in order to achieve the goal (Dettmer, 2011). Because it is expressed as a hierarchical tree, sub-goals for departments or functional areas can be accommodated while achieving alignment to reach an overall goal.

The goal of an organisation is often taken for granted and, if not stated and agreed on explicitly, can lead to constant tensions, even in a commercial setting. In not-for-profit contexts, identifying the goal can be even more challenging. Dettmer (2011) argues that it is the role of the system owner(s) to define the goal, but as this was a desk research project we needed to take this step. Forming a GT for this case forced us to take think more broadly, from a higher standpoint, to identify the big goal (and whose goal?) and what is necessary to achieve that goal.

An illustrative GT for this case study is presented in Figure 9. The protagonists in the case include the medics in the struggling health system, the supermarkets who want to stay in business, and people who want to be happy in their everyday lives. The goal needs to be stated at a high enough level to include all these. We have adopted the term ‘NZ’s overall wellbeing’ as in the EC, though this goal needs to be qualified as relating to alcohol sales in NZ. This is consistent with Forgie et al. (2021, p. 525). When listing the CSFs and NCs, we identify the factors that are critically needed from the perspective of alcohol sales and consumption. They purposefully avoid trying to be an exhaustive list, in line with TOC as a focusing method (Goldratt, 2010).

The goal is stated as follows: Maximise NZ’s overall wellbeing, which requires that we achieve three CSFs relating to wellbeing in three areas: social, physical and economic. The current levels of alcohol-related harm are adversely impacting on all three. The NCs below the CSFs capture both the health-related and supermarkets’ business success factors that need to be met. Traffic-light colour coding (after Hohmann, 2014) can be used by the system owners to indicate areas that they consider are being met (green/thick borders), partly met (orange/thin borders) and not met (red/dashed borders). For our desk study, the two NCs relating to alcohol have been marked as not being met (red/dashed borders) based on the concerns raised in the case. The CSFs and other NCs above them are therefore only partly met. Interestingly, while supermarkets continue to argue they need to continue to sell alcohol to remain profitable, constructing the GT made us question this claim under the current duopolistic market conditions and well-documented excess profits; hence we have assigned green colour coding. The lowest level of the tree reveals some apparent conflicts in meeting the CSFs—for example, regarding pay rates, product range offerings and pricing structures.

#### CQ2: What to change to?

##### TOC Evaporating Cloud—resolution

After depicting the conflict as an EC as in Figure 8, we can then seek to break the conflict by generating ideas for win–win solutions that achieve both requirements B and C, rather than arguing over the conflicting means, D and D′. (We call these ‘injections’—like an injection to cure an illness).

Using the EC method, we identified assumptions underlying each of the arrows: (eg the arrow AB from B to A), and Box A, and came up with many ideas for win–win solutions. Some are shown in Table 3. We then select the injection that appears to be the most promising from the EC analysis for the next part of the process. See Cox, Mabin & Davies 2005, Dettmer, 2007, or Mabin et al., 2008/2009 for more explanation of the EC.

From the above list of possible injections, we chose: Ban alcohol sales in supermarkets but allow them to set up a separate liquor store nearby. This injection is chosen from the list in Table 3 as it is a well-defined idea that seems clearcut and promising, on the surface anyway, as it appears it would reduce harm while also allowing supermarkets to remain in business. It is an approach ‘used successfully in Australia’, which is often used as a benchmark in NZ.

Any solution will have possible downsides so, as part of the TOC TP, we test this ‘solution’, or any other proposal, before considering how to implement, which is done next.

##### FRT and negative branch reservation process: testing/improving the solution

The next step in the TOC TP is to thoroughly investigate the solution proposed in the previous step, before it is implemented. The FRT (and the negative branch reservation (NBR) as part of the FRT) is used to check whether the proposed solution will work as envisaged and to identify and deal with any negative side effects before they occur. The FRT can be built by starting with the CRT and inserting the injection at its base and working upwards, checking that the injection turns negative effects into positive effects.

When testing a solution, we should identify both the expected positive effects and potential negative effects that could occur if the idea was implemented. As the proposer of a solution, it is natural to display confirmation bias (Kahneman, 2011) emphasising the positive effects, while brushing aside the negative effects that will come about if we adopt this solution. On the other hand, when we hear of a proposed change, we are more likely to say, ‘Yes but …’ and list all the potential negatives of the idea. If these negative effects are not dealt with, the solution is bound to fail.

The NBR process is used to surface such potential negative side effects of a change, in order to build a more robust change proposal (Cox et al., 2012; Goldratt, 1994). While originally designed as part of the FRT, the NBR is also effective as a standalone tool. It is used to ‘test drive’ a solution, ensuring that preventive measures are taken to stop potential negative effects from occurring, while ensuring the positive effects of the proposal are realised. Negative side effects are seldom dealt with constructively if this or a similar process is not invoked. They will usually be dismissed as ‘resistance to change’ that needs to be overcome.

Khaw (2005) explains the NBR process as follows: Understand the idea, Understand why it needs improving, and Understand how to improve the idea.

In order to understand the idea, first the chosen idea from the EC is expressed as a clear action statement: ‘Ban alcohol sales in supermarkets but allow supermarkets to set up a separate liquor store’. The following positive and negative effects of this proposal were identified.

Three positives (Desirable Effects)

DE1: Alcohol is no longer viewed as an everyday item

DE2: Everyday alcohol consumption is reduced

DE3: Customers can still choose to purchase alcohol reasonably conveniently

Three negatives (New UnDesirable Effects)

UDE1: Supermarkets make less profit

UDE2: Consumption of alcohol increases

UDE3: Alcohol-related harm gets worse

To understand why the idea needs to be improved, we build the ‘negative branch’ using sufficiency logic to explain why we believe the idea will lead to the listed negative effects.

This then allows us to understand how to improve the idea, by adding extra actions to prevent or mitigate these negative effects while ensuring the positive effects occur. The resulting tree is a ‘Future Reality Tree’ (or, if it is small, a ‘branch’), which depicts how our proposal along with these supporting actions will lead to our desired future.

These are illustrated for the above example. The NBR detailing the feared negative effects is shown in Figure 10, while the Future Reality Branch (FRB) (Figure 11) shows the desired future where the negative effects have been prevented by adding further injections. Both trees are read from the bottom of the tree using ‘IF … AND … THEN …’ as in the CRT, where an ellipse across a group of incoming arrows is read ‘AND’. The links are created by predicting outcomes based on intuition and experience: IF we take this action THEN this effect will occur. Most times the single cause–effect link is not enough on its own without adding a reason or assumption on the side. For example, entities labelled 35 and 37 have been added to explain why 30 leads to 40.<sup>iv</sup> These entities on the side provide leverage points which can be challenged to develop a better solution, even those generally considered to be ‘facts of life’.

One of the new undesired effects we explored was that a separate store could actually lead to increased consumption of alcohol. This can be seen in the left-hand branch: IF customers visit the liquor store to purchase alcohol, AND the separate store next door involves another stop taking more time/effort AND when people have to go to more effort, they usually spend more, THEN customers purchase more alcohol, AND IF that AND people tend to consume what they’ve purchased, THEN alcohol consumption increases, and alcohol-related harm gets worse. Severe restrictions would be needed to offset this, which is done in the next step (see Figure 11).

Some surprising side effects were surfaced, that would need to be dealt with, otherwise they will impede the desired results, including the following:

- If children are not allowed in the liquor store, then children would be left alone in cars while parents shop.

- More people will resort to other drugs like cannabis and harder drugs, if alcohol sales in supermarkets are banned.

Other suggestions, either as primary or supporting injections, included the following:

- Launch education/marketing campaigns.

- Ban sale of high-percentage alcoholic drinks.

- Tightly control sales of alcohol in supermarkets.

- Introduce set prices and raise taxes on alcohol purchased in supermarkets.

- A few students suggested that marijuana should be legalised (as they argue it is less harmful).

### TOC analysis (Change Question 3)

#### CQ3: How to cause the change?

##### TOC Prerequisite Tree (PRT): implementing the change

Having decided on the best solution(s), there will undoubtedly be objections raised, and if these are not identified and overcome they will pop up later as excuses when the implementation fails. TOC’s PRT is used to design a successful implementation.

When planning an implementation using the PRT, we first state the target clearly, then compile a list of obstacles that stand in the way of achieving this target. We then identify an action or condition that would get over each obstacle. These are called Intermediate Objectives (IO). Finally, we sequence these IOs in prerequisite order.

The approach will be illustrated for the Primary Injection (Ban supermarket sales but allow a separate store nearby), which can be replicated as needed for other interventions like marketing campaigns, tax changes or limiting hours of sale. The target, possible obstacles and suggested IOs are provided in Table 4.

The PRT in Figure 15 shows the sequence in which the actions (IOs) need to be achieved. Obstacles are attached to remind us why IOs need to be done. The PRT is read using necessary condition logic. As implementation starts at the bottom, we can read as IO5: Health lobbyists must use social media to put pressure on liquor industry to show more corporate social responsibility (CSR), in order to get over the obstacle (O5) that the liquor lobby strongly opposes any restrictions. We must also do the other three IOs (4, 6a and 6b to get over Obstacles 4 and 6) before we can achieve IO1: Parliament changes the legislation, to get over the obstacle (O1) that the current legislation allows alcohol sales in supermarkets. We must achieve IO1 before IO2: Supermarkets make it fairly easy to get to the new store, to get over the obstacle (O2) that customers want the convenience of buying alcohol in supermarkets. We need to complete IO7 and IO3 as well in order to reach our target: Ban alcohol sales in supermarkets but allow them to set up a store nearby.

##### TOC Transition Tree

Further TOC tools that can help with implementation include the Transition Tree (TrT) and Critical Chain for providing detailed instructions and documentation, and project plans, respectively (see Cox & Schleier, 2010). The TrT is a logic tree that uses sufficiency logic to map the sequence of detailed actions and their rationales that are required to move an organisation from its current reality to the desired future reality as specified in the FRT. Scheinkopf (1999, 2010) describes the method and provides examples of its use. Dettmer (2007, p. 294 ff.) suggests that for most people the TrT is superfluous, and a robust PRT is more than adequate. Further, many times implementation becomes a project for which TOC’s Critical Chain (CCPM) method is more suited (Dettmer, 2007, p. 300 ff.; Goldratt, 1997).

### Implementation and organisational learning

For change to become permanent, management needs to adopt the right measures and reward systems to encourage behaviours aligned with the goal. TOC’s STT can be used to provide a grand plan, encompassing all the individual TOC TP tools (see Scheinkopf, 2010). Another cycle of the framework may need to be undertaken. Because this was a classroom exercise, this stage is not demonstrated. Similarly, other suggestions for supporting the implementation and organisational learning phase of the SD modelling process are not demonstrated here. Indeed, the analysis illustrated here could be repeated in practice, with input from an appropriate range of stakeholders from the wider NZ community (see, e.g., Figure 4 and Table 2).

## SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

### Combined framework

The combined framework (Figure 3) utilises the complementary strengths of the different Qual SD and TOC tools, providing a suitable multi-method approach for tackling complex problems. The methods combine divergent thinking at the start and convergent thinking at the end. They combine sufficiency (cause and effect) and necessity (in order to … we must …) logic to provide a stronger understanding of the reasons behind relationships, as a basis for decision making. The framework provides a mix of holistic, big-picture thinking and detailed analysis, to open up conversation and encourage participation.

The six stages in the framework lead though a structured change process, harnessing resistance to change by incorporating stakeholder concerns and feedback throughout the process, to develop robust and acceptable solutions. Each tool has its own distinct purpose focusing on a different part of the analysis and implementation. The contributions of each tool and stage of the combined multi-method framework demonstrated in the illustrative case analysis are briefly summarised next.

#### Preliminary analysis

A summary of the case situation and an initial review of the undesirable effects set the scene for identifying the specific problems and issues that have led to the systems thinking intervention.

#### Initial Qual SD analysis

This involved establishing an organising question to help set the focus and boundaries of the case analysis, and to identify of the main stakeholders and their interests. This was followed by an indicative BOT chart displaying the main variables of interest under current policy conditions, clearly showing the adverse trends in alcohol consumption, hazardous drinking and associated health costs, resulting in a great part from the heavy price discounting and increasing normalisation of alcohol sales in NZ supermarkets. An initial CLD was constructed based on insights obtained from the identification of variables and causal relationships from the UDE analysis and situation summary. An analysis of the main feedback loops led to identification of a limits-to-growth systems archetype (illustrating the role of government policy in limiting consumption and sales of alcohol in NZ) and a couple of current key leverage points (e.g., alcohol taxes and supermarket alcohol sales prices).

#### TOC analysis (Change Questions 1 and 2)

What to change? The problems evident in the case, listed earlier as UDEs, were connected into a Current Reality Tree (CRT) linking the UDEs to their root causes that need changing. While the CLD from Qual SD also maps cause-and-effect relationships, the logic in the TOC diagrams is more structured, and provides a way of explicitly including the reasons why, and the circumstances under which, such cause–effect relationships hold. The TOC approach posits that the core problem(s) identified by the CRT are usually the result of unresolved (often long-standing) conflicts or dilemmas. An Evaporating Cloud (EC) is constructed to understand why a conflict or dilemma exists, and to surface underpinning assumptions in order to develop breakthrough solutions. This was illustrated with the dilemma posed in the case narrative over whether to ban sales of alcohol in supermarkets or allow supermarkets to continue selling alcohol as an everyday consumable item. In our case, a range of ECs from different stakeholder perspectives were constructed and used when constructing the Goal Tree (GT). The GT provides a high-level strategic map, identifying what is ideal, and highlighting gaps that need addressing. If the GT is constructed at the outset, it can provide a starting point for constructing a Current Reality Tree, linking the gaps to their root causes that need changing.

What to change to? The EC helps decide what to change to. Once the conflict or dilemma has been depicted as an EC, numerous solution ideas can be produced by challenging the assumptions underpinning the cloud. The most promising idea was chosen—in this case, a separate store nearby.

Proposed solutions are tested by building a Future Reality Tree, including the Negative Branch Reservation process. In particular, the NBR looks for negative side effects of any proposed solution and identifies extra actions needed to prevent them, while realising the positive effects of the solution. This analysis surfaced many potential pitfalls of that solution, and proposed necessary actions to mitigate them, as shown in the Future Reality Branch/Tree. In doing so, several supporting injections were identified as being needed to help the primary injection achieve the desired outcomes (and avoid potential negative side effects).

#### Qual SD analysis revisited

From the GT analysis, the initial CLD was further expanded by the inclusion of the overall wellbeing goals relevant to NZ society. Following this, the CLD was further enhanced and new leverage points and feedback loops were identified and analysed. The suggested policy interventions considered the restructuring of supermarket store sales of alcohol and a number of injections suggested by the FRB analysis, including increasing alcohol taxes, setting customer alcohol purchasing limits, setting minimum alcohol sale prices, and increasing warnings related to the harmful effects of alcohol. The revised BOT charts presented the hypothetical future dynamic behaviour over time of the main variables of interest, based on the revised CLD and policy impacts.

#### TOC analysis (Change Question 3)

How to cause the change? Finally, the PRT developed an implementation plan for achieving the proposed solutions. Even good solutions will face obstacles and objections which need to be acknowledged and overcome in order to implement the solution successfully. Further TOC tools are available if needed (TrT and CC) but the PRT is very effective at harnessing resistance to change and helping stakeholders embrace the implementation.

#### Implementation and organisational learning

As the case outlined in this paper was an illustrative teaching exercise (rather than a project with actual stakeholders), the TOC and Qual SD methods for illustrating this stage were not demonstrated.

Our proposed systems thinking change process combines Qual SD and TOC tools, to provide a combined framework for analysing and resolving ill-structured complex problems. The TOC methods complement the Qual SD methods by providing a more in-depth interrogation of the situation and possible solutions by analysing the effects of the proposal and addressing issues that are likely to arise during implementation. The TOC tools surface assumptions that would likely have remained implicit and unchallenged. They use both necessity (in order to … we must …) and sufficiency (if … then …) logic to capture such assumptions. TOC’s logic protocols and checks should be used to scrutinise the TOC diagrams, and can also be used for SD diagrams and models (Balderstone, 1999).

While the methods add value individually, they are more beneficial when used together as findings from the tools mutually inform each other, leading to a fuller understanding. The tools can be used in sequence or iteratively, utilising the entire suite or selected tools in a mix-and-match fashion. The sequence of TOC tools is designed so that omissions or errors in one diagram are corrected in subsequent diagrams. While Dettmer (2007) recommends the GT be done first, in practice it may more naturally evolve as one develops greater understanding. The intermeshing of the Qual SD and TOC tools also benefits from iteration such as with the CLD before and after the GT and NBR.

### Complementarities illustrated in the case

Overall, the Qual SD tools provided a macro view of the supermarket situation in NZ regarding the sales of alcohol, whereas the TOC analysis provided specific suggestions and analyses for improving the situation regarding alcohol sales and general wellbeing of New Zealanders. In this way the tools are complementary, leading to a more holistic and comprehensive assessment of the supermarket situation, and thus leading to a higher likelihood that the situation can be improved for all New Zealanders.

In this particular case, we noted how the Qual SD covers the health, social and normalisation/culture aspects, whereas the TOC tools work towards developing and implementing a solution. As is typical with a complex (wicked) problem (Rittel & Webber, 1973), it was clear there was no single silver bullet solution. Extra injections and actions are needed to prevent negative side effects and overcome obstacles in implementation. Furthermore, it is necessary to canvass views from a range of stakeholders and incorporate their feedback into the solution design and implementation, and avoid tunnel vision (e.g., alcohol-related harm only). For example, the ECs constructed by the students revealed contrasting attitudes towards alcohol, at a number of levels, from those who considered the societal issues to those who raised personal perspectives and concerns such as the need to be able to purchase alcohol whenever and wherever they wanted. The supermarkets and liquor industry face their own dilemmas as well, such as those raised at the end of the case (Appendix A).

The analysis performed here is illustrative, and could be developed in more depth with a wide range of stakeholders before a definitive solution can be recommended. When refining and implementing a solution, modellers can benefit from the TOC approach which, by design, harnesses resistance throughout the change process in order to design and implement successful solutions (Mabin et al., 2001). The diversity of views raised by students in their own analyses when using these methods highlights the value of Qual SD and TOC in assisting us to canvass views and deal with possible negative side effects to reach a robust solution that will be accepted widely.

This was more than just the location and conditions of alcohol sales. There is a much wider picture, captured only by canvassing views of many stakeholders. The Qual SD tools encourage this wider view in a divergent thinking mode, while the TOC tools tend to focus the analysis, encouraging convergent thinking to identify core causes and develop workable and implementable solutions. The combined change process framework helps policy makers think through and refine significant changes before committing to them.

### Value of the integration of both approaches in a teaching context

Student input into the diagrams and analysis above has been sought and gained in a variety of ways. The particular case study has been used as an exam case, assignment case, tutorial case and teaching case, as well as conference presentations. Students’ ideas have been assimilated into the analysis above. When used for teaching, student ideas put forward in Zoom chats can be incorporated into worked diagrams, such as listing obstacles for the Prerequisite Tree developed in class interactively with students. Using the case as a coursework assignment provided scope for individual perspectives to be more fully developed, for example with ECs revealing a variety of levels of analysis from national through to very personal perspectives.

Student feedback has revealed that students value both sets of tools to help them think through complex problems, helping them structure and organise their thinking to reveal conclusions they would not have arrived at by System I thinking (Kahneman, 2011). Moreover, they value the different focuses of each of the tools and the way they work together, and the practical nature of the methods to help in the different stages of analysis.

While the use of TOC in our undergraduate classes has typically been limited to the GT, EC, NBR/FRB and PRT, along with the constraint management approach in The Goal, our postgraduate students have made much fuller use of the TP tools. Moore (2009), Kimani (2015), Johnson (2021) and Youssef (2023) have used GT, CRT, EC, NBR/FRT and PRT. Moore and Johnson have used the TOC tools alongside CLD modelling, gaining valuable insights from using both approaches in complementary fashion in their research.

### Limitations of the analysis

The illustrative case analysis described has been performed as a student learning exercise, without consulting an appropriate sample from the full list of stakeholder groups identified, and our results are therefore illustrative and limited. While the analyses have been rigorous and comprehensive as an academic exercise, we cannot categorically stand by the specific conclusions. We would need to get more stakeholders involved and perform more detailed and informed analyses with them. Despite these limitations, it is apparent that even this simplified process can lead to a more robust long-term solution, especially when carried out with stakeholders, than either Qual SD or TOC on its own.

### Extensions

Future work extending the research outlined in this paper could involve broadening the systems thinking change process (i.e., the framework) to combine TOC tools and thinking processes with the development, analysis and implementation of SD simulation models. In addition, Balderstone’s (1999) suggestion of utilising the TOC Categories of Legitimate Reservation for checking and testing SD systems thinking diagrams and simulation models could be further developed and applied. We see considerable scope and benefits of continuing to incorporate TOC’s rigorous tools and thinking processes into the full SD modelling process. These complementary methods can be evaluated using the framework outlined by Midgley et al. (2013).

It is hoped that these pluralistic and complementary systems thinking approaches will continue to produce enhanced mutual benefits and more in-depth insights and robust long-term solutions for solving difficult problems. This is particularly relevant in the increasingly more complex and uncertain international environments that people, communities and societies are having to face to survive, in a world becoming more dominated by global pandemics, international conflicts and severe climate change.

Finally, we would like to emphasise that the illustrative case outlined in this paper, related to alcohol sales in NZ supermarkets, could be further developed with actual stakeholders in NZ using the Qual SD and TOC methods discussed. In addition, the analysis could be extended by developing quantitative SD models for alcohol policy analysis as outlined, for example, in Cavana and Tobias (2008) and Tobias et al. (2010) analysing scenarios for tobacco policy in New Zealand.

### A.1. Case study: ‘Supermarkets slammed as “mega-drug dealers” as doctors back calls for an end to booze sales’—Matt Stewart (adapted by Vicky Mabin and Bob Cavana for classroom use)

The Medical Association has added its voice to calls for an end to supermarket sales of ‘booze’ (alcoholic drinks such as wine and beer), saying supermarkets discount alcohol more heavily than liquor stores do. ‘Based on evidence, the lower prices stimulate demand and facilitate heavier consumption, while putting it next to milk and bread gives it the impression of a daily consumed staple.’

Medical Association chairwoman Kate Baddock said doctors wanted to blunt the availability of cut-price alcohol, which blighted so many people’s lives.

‘Alcohol causes diseases in ordinary people. Not only does it cause harm in the liver, but it’s associated with an increase in breast and bowel cancer as well as cancers of the mouth, throat, stomach and liver.’ The Association wants to set minimum prices for alcohol, as well as higher taxes, to stop supermarkets discounting alcohol, and to reduce young people’s exposure to it.

Alcohol Health Watch director Nicki Jackson said the powerful liquor lobby— the big breweries and ‘supermarket monopolies’—stifled any attempt by councils (city councils and other local bodies) to introduce alcohol harm reduction policies, by smothering them with legal challenges that resulted in watered-down compromises. ‘Booze is the most harmful drug by far—and yet we put it in our supermarkets,’ she said. ‘It’s more hazardous than tobacco, heroin or methamphetamine, but we still put our focus on reforms surrounding illicit drugs, rather than alcohol.’

Doug Sellman, Director of the National Addiction Centre, said stopping the ‘mega-drug dealing business’ of selling booze in supermarkets would be a giant leap in reducing alcohol-related harm. Banning liquor from supermarkets would ‘curb the activity of these drug dealers and would be a huge step forwards in changing the heavy-drinking culture. One of the barriers to change is the normalisation of alcohol as an ordinary consumable product, rather than us all seeing it clearly for what it really is: a psychoactive drug that is a toxic substance to human tissue, including it being a carcinogen (cancer-causing), as well as an aggressogenic (aggression-inducing) drug as much as methamphetamine is.’

There have been several law changes which have led to the current situation. In 1989, the law was changed making it legal for supermarkets to sell wine. In 1999, the drinking age was lowered to 18, and supermarkets were allowed to sell beer as well as wine. Moreover, a historic review of liquor laws in 2009–10 did not take the step of recommending an end to supermarket sales—which, according to Sellman, was one of the greatest regrets of Sir Geoffrey Palmer, who led the review.

The two large supermarket chains in NZ, Countdown and Foodstuffs, refuted the Medical Association’s claims. Countdown spokeswoman Kate Porter said health statistics showed alcohol consumption had been decreasing over time. Beer and wine made up around 10% of supermarket sales, and Countdown was also seeing a trend towards lower-alcohol options in both beer and wine. Foodstuffs head of external relations Antoinette Laird said there were many restrictions on where alcohol could be located, such as in a single area of a supermarket, as well as how it could be advertised. She disputed the Medical Association’s claim that supermarket alcohol was discounted more heavily than in liquor stores.

However, Statistics NZ data reveal a mixed picture of trends. Data for 2016 showed that the total volume of alcohol ‘available for consumption’ rose 4.2%, to 474 million litres. While the figures do not measure actual consumption, ‘available for consumption’ = production + imports − exports, which is a good proxy for actual consumption. When adjusted for per capita consumption, the alcohol available per person over 15 peaked in 2010 and has been trending down in recent years, as claimed by Countdown. On the other hand, Countdown’s claim regarding lower-alcohol options is not borne out by Statistics NZ data, which shows a trend towards increased consumption of high-alcohol-content beer.

In some parts of New Zealand, alcohol cannot be sold in supermarkets. Invercargill’s mayor, Tim Shadbolt, said the city’s model should be adopted nationally. Their model, by which a licensing trust has a monopoly on all sales, had a downside in that it could disappoint tourists, but he thought it reduced problems of public drinking. ‘Trust control would be a good model for New Zealand. The benefits outweigh the disadvantages,’ Shadbolt said. Other countries have adopted measures to reduce, limit or prevent availability; for example, in Australia alcoholic drinks are sold in a separate store next to the supermarket.

Wellington Medical Officer of Health (at Hutt Valley District Health Board), Stephen Palmer, said the sale of alcohol in supermarkets had certainly changed the drinking scene, and triggered the rise of ‘pre-loading’ (drinking alcohol bought from supermarkets etc. before going to bars where alcohol is more expensive). ‘It would be nice to wind the clock back, but I think it’s wishful thinking.’

Wellington bar owner Matt McLaughlin, who has worked for breweries and supermarkets, said he backed the idea of getting alcohol off supermarket shelves. ‘I’ve seen the supermarkets buying booze in 24-pallet loads, getting the top discount and it hitting the floor as a loss leader. So it’s no surprise that the public has a perception that alcohol is overpriced in bars.’

A police spokesperson said: ‘Police work with the council, the hospitality industry and partner agencies with a focus on reducing alcohol-related harm.’ Under the Sale and Supply of Alcohol Act, it is the role of police to enforce legislation; it is the role of the government of the day to set the legislation.

### B.2. Brief definitions of selected TOC terms

See Cox et al. (2012) TOCICO Dictionary (2nd edition) for full definitions.

Injection—(i) action that invalidates an assumption to resolve a conflict in an EC; (ii) action, state or condition to address or prevent an undesirable effect. Injections are expressed as a full sentence and contained in a square-cornered box when in an FRT/FRB.

Primary injection—the main injection at the base of the FRT or FRB. Supporting injection—an extra injection added in an FRT/FRB if required to support the primary injection achieve the desired outcomes.

Entity—equivalent to a variable in CLD but must be a full sentence and satisfy the CLR. An entity is contained in a rounded-rectangle box in a tree.

Ellipse (or banana)—‘and’ connector; used in trees using SC logic (CRT, FRT/FRB, TrT). See section on CRT for usage.

## APPENDIX C

CATEGORIES OF LEGITIMATE RESERVATION (CLR): A SELF-SCRUTINY CHECKLIST ADAPTED FROM DETTMER (2007, p. 65)

The first three are stated as desired attributes of entities and relationships.

1. CLARITY (seeking to understand)

Is the meaning/context of words (in each entity) unambiguous? Would I add any verbal explanation if reading the tree to someone else? If so, add the explanation (in the entity or as a separate entity).

Is the connection between cause and effect convincing ‘at face value’?

(If not, are intermediate steps missing? Then consider splitting into two or more entities)

2. ENTITY EXISTENCE (complete, properly structured, valid statements of cause or effect)

Is it a complete sentence? Does it make sense?

Is it free of embedded ‘if–then’ statements? (Look for ‘… because …’ and ‘… in order to …’)

Does it convey only one idea? (not a compound entity) Does it exist in my (or someone else’s) reality? Can it be documented with evidence?

3. CAUSALTY EXISTENCE (logical connection between cause and effect) Does an ‘if–then’ connection really exist? Does the proposed cause, if fact, result in the stated effect? Does it make sense when read aloud exactly as written?

Is the cause tangible? (If not, look for a confirming additional predicted effect)

The following five categories describe errors to look for and correct:

4. CAUSE INSUFFICIENCY (a non-trivial dependent element is missing) Can the cause, as stated, result in the effect on its own? If not, look for significant causal factors that are missing, and add.

Is/are the written cause(s) sufficient to justify all parts of the effect(s)? If not, add the extra cause(s).

Is an ellipse required? (to connect causes that are required jointly to cause the effect).

5. ADDITIONAL CAUSE (a separate, independent cause that produces the same effect)

- Is there anything else that might cause the same effect on its own? If so, add the ‘additional cause’ (without an ellipse).

If the stated cause is eliminated, will the effect still remain because of another cause? If so, show that ‘additional cause’, without an ellipse.

6. CAUSE–EFFECT REVERSAL (effect is misstated as the cause; arrow pointing in the wrong direction)

Is the stated effect really the cause, and the stated cause really the effect? If so, the cause–effect arrow is the wrong way around.

Is the stated cause really a reason why, or instead how we know the effect exists? If the latter, then reverse the arrow.

7. PREDICTED EFFECT EXISTENCE (additional corroborating effect resulting from the cause)

Is the cause intangible? If so, look for other unavoidable outcomes of the proposed cause besides the stated effect, to confirm the cause.

8. TAUTOLOGY (circular logic)

Is the cause intangible?

Is the effect offered as the rationale for the existence of the cause? For example, what else could it be?

Are there unavoidable outcomes identifiable besides the proposed effect?
